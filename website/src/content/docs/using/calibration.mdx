A page printer rarely prints exactly where the page says. The paper moves a little in the tray, the print is a fraction too large or too small, and a sheet of labels leaves no room for that: a print that is half a millimetre off already touches the edge of a label. **Calibration** measures how far a tray is off. Labelyard then moves every print from that tray by the opposite.

You do this once per printer and tray, and again when you change the paper or the tray. It takes about ten minutes. Open **Calibration** in the web app; it walks you through three steps: print the calibration page, enter the deviation, check the result.

## What you need

- The page printer, added as a **Page printer (IPP)** with its trays. See [Printers](/install/printers/#page-printers).
- A real sheet of the sheet type you will print on. Print on the sheet itself, not on plain paper: the cuts of the labels are what you measure against.
- The correct **sheet type**. If its measurements are wrong, the calibration cannot be right; see [Sheets and layouts](/using/sheets/).

## Step 1: Print the calibration page

Choose the printer, the tray and the sheet type, then press **Print calibration page** (or download the PDF and print it yourself). The page shows the cut lines of the sheet type and a small scale at every crossing between four labels.

Writing a number on each cell, row by row, is optional. It helps when you take photos of the sheet.

## Step 2: Enter the deviation

### One crossing

<div class="ly-guide-figure">
<Crossing role="img" aria-label="How to read one crossing" />
</div>

1. The two edges of the gap between two columns: the sides of the labels.
2. The middle of that gap (dash-dot line). The printed line should lie here.
3. The cut between two rows.

- Heavy solid line: the printed **S line**. Heavy dashed line: the printed **Z line** (dotted on a sheet without a gap between the rows, as in the drawing). S counts the gaps between columns, Z the gaps between rows.
- The numbers on the scale are the value to enter when the middle of the gap (across) or the cut (down) falls there.
- The scale lies on both lines: 0 on the printed line, a long tick every 1 mm, a short tick every 0.2 mm, up to ±2 mm.
- Example: the middle of the gap lies 0.6 mm left of the printed line and the cut 0.4 mm above it. The scale reads +0.6 and +0.4 there, so across is +0.6 and down is +0.4.

At a crossing, look where the middle of the gap (across) or the cut (down) falls on the scale, and count its distance from the printed line. Between two ticks, estimate to 0.1 mm by eye. The signs are:

- **+ across** = the ink lies right of the middle of the gap, **− across** = left of it.
- **+ down** = the ink lies below the cut, **− down** = above it.

If the sheet has no gap between the rows, the Z line sits on the cut. Peel off every other label (checkerboard) only if the cut is hard to see.

### Which crossings

Read at least the four outer crossings and the one in the centre. The drawing shows them for a sheet with 4 columns and 12 rows; the web app draws the one of your sheet type.

1. S1 · Z1
2. S3 · Z1
3. S1 · Z11
4. S3 · Z11
5. S2 · Z6 (centre)

The numbers in the drawing follow this list. S counts the gaps between columns from the left, Z the gaps between rows from the top.

### Enter the readings

Enter the readings in the **readings helper**: choose the crossing for each, type the across and down values (a decimal comma or point both work) and press **Fit**. Labelyard fits the offset, the scale and the rotation of the tray to your readings and fills the values in below. Nothing is saved yet: check the values, then save them. A reading that does not fit the others is marked **check this reading**; read that crossing again.

On a sheet with only two columns or two rows all crossings lie on one line, so the readings cannot be fitted and the readings helper is not offered. Enter the values directly in the form below it.

## Step 3: Check the result

Print the check page. It is the same page, now with the correction. If every crossing is within ±0.5 mm, you are done. If not, measure again and adjust.

## Printer or sheet type?

When the page is still off, the deviation tells you where to look. Compare what you see with this table:

| What you see | Cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The same shift in one direction everywhere, on every sheet type | Offset of the printer or tray | Calibrate; the correction removes it |
| The deviation grows with the distance, in both directions and on every sheet type | Scale of the printer (it prints too large or too small) | Calibrate |
| The deviation grows differently downwards than sideways, and differs left and right | Rotation (the printer prints skewed) | Calibrate |
| The same shift or a drift on one sheet type only, while other sheets sit with the same correction | Measurements of the sheet type (margin, pitch, label height) | Correct the sheet type (add a new version), not the printer |
| A jump between two neighbouring gaps | Sheet type (gap, number of columns or rows) | Correct the sheet type |
| A sideways shift that differs from sheet to sheet | Paper feed | Push the sheet against the guide and print a second one; change the correction only if it repeats |
| An even remainder below about 0.2 mm | Reading accuracy | Do nothing |

A wrong pitch does not show as a jump but as a steady drift, much like a printer scale error. That is why the sheet types matter: if one type drifts while the others sit with the same correction, look at that type. One real case was a sheet type whose row pitch was 9.98 instead of 10.0 mm.

The check print follows the same rule: every crossing within ±0.5 mm is done. A remainder that is larger, or that grows towards one edge, is one of the rows above.

### Reading tips

- Read across and down separately at every crossing, each as a number. "Looks centred" is not a reading.
- Read crossings in the first, middle and last row and column; otherwise a drift stays hidden.
- If the scale is hard to read, the known gap width (column pitch minus label width) works as a ruler.
- Vendor data sheets can be off. Measure a pitch with a ruler over ten labels and divide by ten.
- For photos: full resolution, zoom into each crossing, and low-angle light makes the cut visible. Bent paper makes lines look curved, so judge at the crossing only.
